Timber and finishes

What you are paying for, said before you sign

The species and the finish are the product. Each card below carries the category and one honest line about how that material ages, how it behaves near the sea and how it is repaired.

Timber

Solid timber

It moves with humidity: it expands and contracts with the season, and the design has to let that movement happen. It can be sanded back and restored as many times as needed. [CONFIRM species and origin documentation]

Timber

Natural veneer

Real grain over a stable substrate: the figure of timber without the movement of solid wood. Repair is more delicate, because the veneer is thin. [CONFIRM thickness and supplier]

Timber

Faced MDF

Stable, flat and predictable — the base of most fitted joinery. Its weak point is the edge and contact with water: sealing and edging decide how long the piece lasts. [CONFIRM range and facing]

Timber

Plywood

Cross-laminated plies: it holds a screw better than MDF and tolerates more moisture, with the exposed edge some pieces use deliberately. [CONFIRM type and glue class]

Finish

Varnish

A film over the timber: it protects well and resists liquids, but when it is scratched the repair is to the whole film, not to the spot. [CONFIRM product and number of coats]

Finish

Oil

It penetrates rather than covers: the timber keeps its feel and a repair stays local. In exchange it asks for periodic maintenance. [CONFIRM reapplication interval]

Finish

Lacquer

A closed colour and an even surface, over panel rather than over visible grain. Touching it up means the piece goes back to the spray booth. [CONFIRM whether there is an in-house spray booth]

Finish

Wax and stain

Wax gives a low sheen and a dry touch; stain changes the colour without hiding the grain. Both are the easiest finishes to touch up at home. [CONFIRM whether they are offered]

Timber on the coast

What salt air does to your piece — and what changes in the specification

A house 200 metres from the sea in Florianópolis is not the same joinery job as a flat in the centre. None of this is opinion: it is specification.

Why this section exists. Every joinery workshop on this island sells “fitted furniture”. Not one answers the single variable that decides whether the piece reaches five years. Answering it is owning the specification conversation.

Humidity that rises and falls every day

Near the sea, relative humidity swings far more than it does inland. Solid timber expands and contracts with it, and the design has to allow that movement: a groove instead of rigid glue, calculated clearances on doors and drawers, and the dimensional reference chosen for the worst case, not the average.

The edge is where the piece dies first

In panel work, water almost never gets in through the face. It gets in through the edge: the top of a door, a sink cut-out, a foot standing on a wet floor. Sealing the edge and applying the banding properly matter more than any colour choice.

Hardware is the component that corrodes

Hinges, runners and handles are metal in salt air. The corrosion class of the hardware is a line in the estimate, not a detail. [CONFIRM which hardware is specified near the sea]

Ventilation behind the unit

A unit pressed against a wall that breathes collects moisture at the back. A standoff and an air path change the working life of the piece more than any varnish does.

Direct sun ages the colour

The face turned to the sun shifts tone before the others. That is not a defect, it is how timber behaves — but it has to be said at the moment of choosing, not two years later.

Repair rather than replacement

A penetrating finish repairs locally; a film repairs as a whole. Anyone living near the sea should be choosing with the repair in mind, not only with how the piece leaves the workshop.

Solid timber or MDF?

There is no single answer: solid timber moves with humidity and recovers with sanding; panel is stable and flat but lives or dies by its edge. Near the sea the decision changes weight — see the section on timber on the coast.

Does timber survive near the sea?

It does, if the specification is written for it: movement clearance, sealed edges, hardware with an adequate corrosion class and an air path behind the piece. Without that, salt air and the humidity swing collect in two or three years.

What is the warranty?

The statutory warranty is 90 days for durable goods and services, under CDC art. 26, II. Anything longer exists only if it is in writing (art. 50). The piece, the installation and the hardware are three separate warranties — the hardware’s is the manufacturer’s and carries its own conditions, which we have not read.
